<Summary>
A commercial tDCS device for depression is expected to get FDA clearance
around September. Its hardware is a constant-current source, two sponge
electrodes, and a headband. I'm going to replicate it at home for roughly
$100, use it on myself under direct supervision from my providers, run the
whole thing as an institution-grade N-of-1, and eventually open-source
everything. This post is the charter — what I'm doing, why this one, and
where the actual risk lives.
</Summary>

## The goal, stated plainly

I have treatment-resistant depression. The escalation path from here is
well known and I've been on parts of it: it runs through TMS and eventually
ECT. My goal is blunt: iterate on cheap, safe, well-measured neurostimulation
until I never have to think about ECT or TMS again — and publish everything
so the next person can start where I finished instead of where I started.

## Why this device is boring (that's the point)

tDCS — transcranial direct current stimulation — pushes about 2 milliamps
of direct current through two saline-soaked sponges on your scalp. That's
it. The device coming to market is, electrically speaking:

- a constant-current source
- two electrodes at fixed positions (roughly F3/F4, over dorsolateral
  prefrontal cortex)
- a fixed protocol: same dose, same duration, same placement, every session
- an app that mostly exists to track adherence and stop you from fiddling

The bill of materials is on the order of $50. The other ~$449 buys FDA
clearance, industrial design, and the app. Those are real things worth real
money — for a company. For one person with a supervising care team, the
$50 version plus rigor can do the same job.

## Why I picked this experiment and not a scarier one

I've thought a lot about the self-experimentation space, and the reason I
landed here is risk tiering, not enthusiasm. Some people synthesize peptides
in their garage and inject mystery molecules with no assay. I am not doing
that, ever. I picked tDCS *because* it's arguably the hardest serious
intervention to hurt yourself with:

- 2mA through scalp-level impedance is a well-characterized, extensively
  studied dose with decades of literature behind it
- the realistic worst case is a skin burn from bad electrode practice —
  dried-out sponges and high current density, not amperage
- the failure mode that actually matters ("what if the device faults and
  delivers more than 2mA?") has a beautifully dumb hardware answer

That last one deserves its own section, because it's the whole safety case.

## The safety case is physics, not software

The question isn't "will I be careful." The question is "how do I make an
overdose physically impossible." The answer: run the current source off a
supply with a low compliance-voltage ceiling. Push current through several
kilohms of head-plus-electrode impedance from a supply that tops out around
12 volts, and Ohm's law caps the deliverable current no matter what the
electronics do. Every transistor in the device can betray me and the
physics still says no.

Hardware-limited beats software-limited. No firmware bug, no app crash, no
"oops" can exceed a ceiling that's enforced by the power supply itself.
That's an afternoon of circuit design, not a regulatory program.

The second pillar I'm stealing from the commercial device is the one
genuinely clever thing about it: it's *fiddle-proof on purpose*. Fixed dose,
fixed placement, fixed duration, no knobs. I am a fiddler by nature, and
this is the one project where fiddling destroys the data. So the build spec
includes building the fiddle-proofing in: fixed protocol, session logging
baked into the device, nothing to adjust.

## Supervision is not optional

I'm not doing this alone in a basement. I'm doing it under direct
supervision from my providers — the same care team that manages the rest of
my treatment. Depression is the one domain where your own outcome data is
least trustworthy to yourself, which is exactly why the measurement layer
matters more than the hardware:

- pre-registered protocol before the first session
- standardized outcome measures (PHQ-9 and friends) on a fixed cadence,
  not vibes
- session logs from the device itself
- my providers in the loop on the data, not just the idea

The device is a weekend of work. The N-of-1 rigor is the actual project.

## What gets open-sourced, and when

Everything, eventually — schematics, firmware, protocol, and the outcome
data. But the self-experiment and the release are separable stages. My
build needs to be safe for *me*, supervised. A published build needs to be
safe for someone with worse judgment than mine who skips the supervision
paragraph. That means the release version gets over-engineered on exactly
the boring parts — fail-safe current limiting, impedance monitoring,
session caps — before any build files go up. That hardening is a later
stage, and it doesn't need to be solved before session one.

## The larger arc (why this isn't a tangent)

This sits inside a longer trajectory: build frontier-grade personal
tooling, publish it, and move step by step toward real medical research —
eventually a lab that takes both patient outcomes and cutting-edge
technology seriously at the same time. The strategy for the device world
is user-research-before-product: become the most rigorous *user* of this
class of device, instrument everything, and let that reveal where the real
gaps are. If a better device deserves to exist someday, the N-of-1 data
will say so louder than any pitch.

Also, a small side pleasure: I keep a running list of the times AI
assistants told me things were impossible, too dangerous, or assumed I
didn't know what I was doing. This project has already added entries. The
list will make a good slide one day.
