# Research 01, the Uber receipt

Feeds the `receipt-uber` slide in the open-weights deck. The slide's job is to
recover a real audience memory: short urban ride-hail trips that could feel like
roughly $10 during the growth-subsidy period, against what that general service
costs now. It is not a literal same-route-across-time comparison. Ride-hail
quotes vary by city, route, time, product, supply, and account, so the numbers
must survive someone checking their scope.

## Deliverables

1. **The $10 era, dated.** What years could a typical short city ride
   (concert home, airport to hotel) actually cost ~$10, and what stage was
   Uber at (pre-IPO, VC-subsidized, growth-over-profit)? A date range plus
   one sentence of company context.
2. **The service now.** A current Uber quote for a representative short urban
   trip. A screenshot from Ben's phone is a useful illustration, but it is not
   a rigorous time series or evidence of the historical price for that route.
   A published aggregate comparison is the evidence for the broader increase.
3. **The increase, quantified.** A citable figure for how much fares rose
   over a defined period. Do not claim the figure isolates the effect of
   subsidies ending unless its source actually does that analysis.

## Leads to verify, not conclusions

- Slate, "The Decade of Cheap Uber Rides Is Over" (May 2022): assess whether
  its price claims trace to the underlying data and preserve the comparison
  period and product scope.
- Mid-2010s reporting that rider fares covered roughly 40% of ride cost: use
  only if a reliable underlying source supports the number. Do not use the
  number merely because it is rhetorically convenient.
- LA Times (May 2019), "Uber fares are cheap, thanks to venture capital."

## Findings

### What makes the $10 memory defensible

- [Uber's February 2015 fare campaign](https://www.uber.com/us/en/newsroom/convenience-has-never-cost-less-2/)
  advertised specific South Florida UberX trips at $10. This is primary
  evidence that short rides in selected U.S. markets could cost roughly $10 in
  the mid-2010s. It does not establish a national average or a typical airport
  trip.
- [UberPOOL's 2015 launch post](https://www.uber.com/us/en/newsroom/uberpool/)
  says its minimum fare was $5 and that it was 35% cheaper than uberX. A 2016
  Uber post says Manhattan commuter-hour uberPOOL was [a flat $5 while average
  uberX was $19.65](https://www.uber.com/us/en/newsroom/how-3-minutes-can-save-you-14-2/).
  The honest date range the linked evidence directly supports is roughly 2015–16,
  especially for short, un-surcharged rides and shared products. Do not present
  $10 as the universal price of an ordinary UberX ride.

### What establishes the growth-subsidy economics

- [Lyft's 2019 S-1](https://investor.lyft.com/financials/sec-filings/content/0001193125-19-077391/d633517ds1a.htm)
  states that a rider's upfront fare can be lower than the payment Lyft has
  committed to a driver, creating a loss on that transaction. This is direct
  company evidence that a ride-hail platform could subsidize a trip. It is not
  an estimate of how often that happened or a claim about every Uber ride.
- [Uber's 2020 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1543151/000154315121000014/uber-20201231.htm)
  says Uber could lose money on a Mobility transaction when a fixed upfront
  rider price was lower than the driver's time-and-distance payment. It also
  reports a $961 million decrease in consumer promotions and says lower
  incentive spending improved Mobility take rate. This is direct evidence that
  Uber sometimes absorbed trip costs and that promotions materially affected
  its economics. It does not measure how often rides lost money or isolate the
  subsidy on a typical $10 ride.
- [Los Angeles Times reporting from 2016](https://www.latimes.com/business/technology/la-fi-tn-future-ride-hailing-snap-story.html)
  describes a competition-driven subsidy battle in which Uber's fares kept
  falling and notes the company was spending heavily to keep fares low. This is
  useful context, but it is reported analysis rather than a company accounting
  line item.

### What establishes later price increases

- [YipitData's U.S. ride-hail analysis](https://www.yipitdata.com/resources/how-is-the-rideshare-driver-supply-problem-now)
  finds Uber and Lyft charges per mile still significantly above January 2021,
  while discounts and vouchers remained well below pre-COVID levels. It gives
  current, industry-data context but not a single percentage in the accessible
  summary.
- The [Los Angeles Times report on YipitData](https://www.latimes.com/business/technology/story/2022-05-03/lyft-shares-plummet-as-company-spends-more)
  states that the average U.S. ride-hail trip was about $20 in 2022 Q1, about
  45% above 2019 Q1. Use this as the cleanest aggregate headline, with the
  scope attached: it covers U.S. ride-hail, not the same route, city, or Uber
  product across time.
- [CNBC's reporting on Rakuten Intelligence data, summarized by Popular
  Science](https://www.popsci.com/technology/uber-lyft-rides-getting-more-expensive/)
  reports that Uber and Lyft ride costs were 92% higher in July 2021 than in
  January 2018. Treat this as a corroborating, pandemic-era measure rather than
  the single deck number: it combines services and its period includes unusual
  COVID supply conditions.

### Best evidence package for Ben to inspect

Read the three primary-company pages above plus the YipitData and Los Angeles
Times links. Use a fresh, timestamped Uber quote only as the present-day visual
example. It should be captioned as a representative quote, not as a historical
comparison. The evidence supports "cheap short rides were a real feature of the
growth era, and aggregate U.S. ride-hail prices later rose sharply." It does
not support a precise claim that one specific route went from $10 to a specific
current price because subsidies ended.
